How to write a clear contractor estimate

A useful estimate explains the work, the price, and what happens next. It gives customers enough detail to make a decision and gives you a shared reference if the job changes.

WWorkCraft AI Field Guide October 10, 2026

Clear estimates are easier to review, compare, and approve. The right amount of detail depends on the job: a small repair may need only a few lines, while a larger project may need phases, materials, and assumptions spelled out.

1. Start with the customer and job

Include the customer’s name, the service address, the date, and a short title for the work. If you use estimate numbers, make the reference easy to find in later conversations. Keep private notes out of the customer-facing proposal.

2. Describe the scope in everyday language

Say what you will do and where. “Replace damaged shingles on the north roof slope and haul away debris” is more useful than “roof repair.” Break separate phases or distinct kinds of work into their own lines so the customer can follow the plan.

3. Show quantities and units that make sense

Use units that match how the work is measured or sold, such as each, hour, linear foot, square foot, sheet, or job. Check that the quantity and unit agree. If a measurement is still an assumption, say so and confirm it before work begins.

4. Choose the right level of price detail

Line items help customers see what is included and can make change discussions clearer. For a simple fixed-price job, a concise scope with one total may be easier to read. Whichever format you use, keep the detailed calculation in your own records and make the customer-facing scope honest and understandable.

5. State important assumptions and exclusions

Note conditions that could affect price or scope, such as hidden damage, access limits, permit fees, disposal, or a material selection the customer has not made yet. Be specific about what is excluded. If an unexpected condition changes the work, pause and agree on a written change before proceeding.

6. Make the total and next step easy to find

Show the subtotal, any markup or tax that applies, the final total, and any deposit or payment terms. State how long the estimate is valid if prices or availability may change. Tell the customer how to approve it and what you will do after approval.

Quick review before you send

  • The customer, address, and work description are correct.
  • Every quantity has the right unit and price.
  • Assumptions, exclusions, tax, and payment terms are clear.
  • The customer knows how to approve and contact you with a question.

An estimate is still your professional judgment. Verify measurements, local requirements, taxes, and current material and labor costs before sharing it.
